# Meta-Review Critique: Cross-Species Cell-Type Annotation Hypotheses

## 1. RECURRING ISSUES

- **Over-reliance on unvalidated homology assumptions without fallback mechanisms:** Every hypothesis assumes high-quality homology mapping (whether one-to-one, non-one-to-one, or phylogenetic), yet reviews consistently flag this as unrealistic for distant species. No hypothesis includes explicit robustness checks or degradation analyses when homology quality drops—a critical blind spot given that the hardest cross-species cases are exactly where homology is least reliable.

- **Adversarial domain alignment is invoked uncritically as a silver bullet:** Three of four hypotheses employ adversarial methods for species-invariant representations, but multiple reviews and debates identify the same unresolved tension—adversarial training can destroy biological signal along with batch effects. No hypothesis provides a principled mechanism to preserve meaningful biological variation while removing species-specific artifacts, treating this as an implementation detail rather than a core design challenge.

- **Benchmarking optimism and data leakage risks are never proactively addressed:** Reviews repeatedly warn that positive results may be misleading due to inadequate baselines, train-test leakage through shared cell types or gene families, and batch effect confounding. Yet no hypothesis proposes rigorous cross-validation strategies (e.g., leave-one-species-out, phylogenetic distance-based held-out clades) or negative controls that would distinguish true generalization from memorization.

- **Empirical grounding is systematically absent across all proposals:** Every hypothesis receives the same verdict in full reviews—"plausible but all claims unaddressed." The generation process produces architectures and mechanisms without anchoring them to specific, named anomalous observations from the literature that existing methods cannot explain. This creates a field of plausible-sounding but unfalsifiable proposals that cannot be meaningfully compared.

- **Modular complexity is conflated with novelty and impact:** Debate outcomes consistently favor hypotheses with more components (graph transformers + adversarial alignment + contrastive learning + non-one-to-one homology), but reviews note that these combinations are "existing ideas" and that simpler baselines may perform equivalently. No hypothesis includes ablation commitments or minimal sufficient model benchmarks, making it impossible to distinguish genuine innovation from complexity stacking.

The mere presence of a decomposition into testable units signals rigor and distinguishes proposals that can be incrementally validated from those requiring all-or-nothing acceptance.

## 3. GAPS IDENTIFIED

- **No hypothesis addresses the fundamental asymmetry in cross-species data availability:** All proposals assume symmetric transfer between species pairs, but real-world annotation needs are highly asymmetric—we have rich atlases for mouse and human but sparse data for most other species. No hypothesis explicitly designs for few-shot or zero-shot transfer from data-rich to data-poor species, nor addresses how performance degrades as the target species' data volume shrinks. This is arguably the most practically important use case.

- **Evolutionary rate heterogeneity across cell types and gene families is entirely unmodeled:** All hypotheses treat cross-species alignment as a uniform problem, but immune cells evolve rapidly while neurons are deeply conserved, and transcription factors diverge differently than metabolic enzymes. No proposal incorporates phylogenetic branch-length information, evolutionary rate estimates, or cell-type-specific conservation priors. This is a glaring biological gap given that the field's central challenge is handling variable conservation.

- **Alternative alignment paradigms beyond latent space matching are unexplored:** Every hypothesis uses some form of representation alignment (adversarial, contrastive, hyperbolic), but no proposal considers fundamentally different approaches such as explicit evolutionary models of cell-type diversification, optimal transport with structured phylogenetic costs, or Bayesian ancestral state reconstruction. The hypothesis space has collapsed onto a single paradigm, leaving potentially more principled approaches entirely unrepresented.

- **The role of gene expression measurement technology differences across species is ignored:** Cross-species comparisons typically use different experimental protocols (droplet-based vs. plate-based, different read depths, different gene capture efficiencies), yet no hypothesis addresses how technical variation interacts with biological variation. This is a known confounder in single-cell benchmarking that will affect any cross-species method but receives zero attention across all proposals.

- **No hypothesis considers the possibility that "universal cell-type annotation" may be an ill-posed problem for some cell types:** The entire hypothesis space assumes that cross-species cell-type homology exists and can be recovered, but some cell types are genuinely species-specific innovations. No proposal includes a mechanism for detecting when a cell type in the query species has no meaningful counterpart in the reference, which is essential for avoiding forced misannotation in real applications.

## 5. REVIEW CALIBRATION

- **Reviewers should penalize absence of failure mode analysis more heavily than absence of supporting evidence.** Current reviews note that "all claims are unaddressed" but still rate hypotheses as "plausible and testable." This creates a floor effect where no hypothesis can fail. Reviewers should instead treat the failure to specify when and how the approach breaks as a fundamental scientific weakness, not a minor omission. A hypothesis without explicit failure conditions is not testable in any meaningful sense.

- **The "observation" review dimension needs sharper criteria to prevent all hypotheses receiving the same neutral assessment.** Currently, observation reviews consistently find that no anomalous observation is directly addressed, yet this finding does not differentiate hypotheses or feed back into generation. Reviewers should be required to state: (a) what observation would constitute the strongest test of this hypothesis, and (b) whether that observation already exists in the literature. This would make the observation criterion discriminative rather than uniformly non-informative.

- **Debate judges should explicitly weigh "simplicity and falsifiability" against "novelty of component combination" to counter the complexity bias.** The consistent pattern of more complex hypotheses winning debates, even when reviews note that all components are established, suggests that novelty assessments are overweighting architectural novelty and underweighting parsimony. Adding a specific simplicity criterion to debate rubrics would rebalance evaluations toward hypotheses that make stronger claims with fewer moving parts.

- **Reviewers should distinguish between "biologically grounded" and "biologically plausible" when assessing assumptions.** Multiple deep verifications note that assumptions are "plausible but lack direct evidence," yet this plausibility is treated as a positive signal. In practice, many plausible-sounding biological assumptions (e.g., "gene programs are conserved") are false for specific cell types or conditions. Reviewers should require proposers to specify the domain of applicability for each assumption and should treat unqualified plausibility claims as insufficiently rigorous.

- **The evaluation of "potential impact" should require a counterfactual: what would we learn if this hypothesis is true that we wouldn't learn from existing methods?** Current impact assessments reward generic promises of "uncovering conserved regulatory modules" without specifying what new biological insight would be enabled. Requiring reviewers to articulate the specific discovery the hypothesis would unlock—and why existing methods cannot deliver it—would prevent inflated impact scores for incremental proposals and would naturally favor hypotheses that address genuine knowledge gaps.

## Research Overview

# Research Overview: Deep Learning Frameworks for Cross-Species Single-Cell Annotation

## Executive Summary

The challenge of transferring cell-type annotations across evolutionarily divergent species represents a fundamental bottleneck in comparative single-cell genomics. Current methods achieve reasonable performance when transferring annotations between closely related species with high-quality one-to-one ortholog mapping, but systematically fail for distant species pairs, asymmetric data scenarios, and cell types with complex evolutionary histories. The five hypotheses evaluated here converge on a critical insight: the path forward requires moving beyond simple gene-level alignment toward modeling the conserved functional modules—gene programs, regulatory grammars, and phylogenetic relationships—that define cell-type identity across species.

Two hypotheses emerge as particularly promising and complementary. The homology-aware graph transformer framework (CrossAnn, Hypothesis 1) directly addresses the well-documented failure of one-to-one ortholog-based methods by modeling non-one-to-one homologous gene mappings within a heterogeneous graph architecture, combined with adversarial domain alignment and semi-supervised contrastive learning. Its strength lies in tackling a specific, named limitation of existing methods with a modular design that enables component-by-component validation. The gene program factorization approach (Hypothesis 2) proposes a mechanistically distinct alternative: learning evolutionarily conserved gene programs as a transferable vocabulary for cell-type classification, shifting the annotation unit from individual genes to functional modules. This hypothesis is distinguished by its biological grounding in the well-established concept of co-regulated gene programs and its potential to generalize to unseen cell types that utilize known programs in novel combinations.

The hyperbolic manifold alignment framework (Hypothesis 3) introduces the theoretically elegant idea that cell-type evolution follows tree-like structures best captured in hyperbolic geometry, though its practical advantages over Euclidean methods require empirical demonstration. The remaining hypotheses (4 and 5) propose valid but less differentiated combinations of established techniques—self-supervised pretraining, graph neural networks, adversarial alignment, and topic models—without clearly articulating what specific failure mode of existing methods they resolve.

Meta-review analysis reveals critical blind spots across all proposals: no hypothesis addresses asymmetric data availability (the practically most important use case), none incorporates evolutionary rate heterogeneity across cell types and gene families, and all assume that cross-species cell-type homology exists and can be recovered without providing mechanisms to detect genuinely species-specific innovations. The most actionable path forward combines the modular validation strategy of Hypothesis 1 with the mechanistic novelty of Hypothesis 2, while explicitly incorporating phylogenetic branch-length information and designing for few-shot transfer from data-rich to data-poor species.

## Top Hypotheses

### Hypothesis 1: Cross-Species Annotation via Homology-Aware Graph Transformer with Adversarial Domain Alignment (CrossAnn)
**Elo: 1576**

**Key Claim:** A heterogeneous graph neural network that explicitly models non-one-to-one homologous gene mappings, combined with transformer-based attention, adversarial species domain alignment, and semi-supervised contrastive learning, will achieve superior cross-species annotation accuracy—particularly for distant species pairs where complex homology relationships are most prevalent.

**Scientific Basis:** This hypothesis directly targets a well-documented limitation: existing methods like scArches and Seurat rely exclusively on one-to-one orthologs, systematically missing the many-to-many homologous relationships that characterize real evolutionary gene family dynamics. CAME demonstrated that incorporating non-one-to-one homology improves cross-species cell-type assignment, but lacks explicit batch correction and multi-omics integration. MoDAmix showed that adversarial adaptation combined with semi-supervised class alignment effectively corrects batch effects. CrossAnn proposes that these complementary strengths can be unified within a single heterogeneous graph framework where nodes represent cells and genes across two species, and edges encode gene homology, cell-gene expression, and cross-species cell similarity. A graph transformer learns embeddings by attending over this structure, an adversarial discriminator removes species-specific variation, and contrastive learning enforces biological consistency using known cell-type labels.

**Experimental Approach:** The proposed evaluation spans three benchmark cross-species atlases (human-mouse pancreas, human-mouse-zebrafish brain, human-mouse kidney) with comparisons against CAME, scArches, Nephrobase Cell+, and one-to-one ortholog baselines. Metrics include annotation accuracy (ARI, F1), batch correction (kBET), biological conservation (silhouette scores), and scalability to million-cell atlases. Crucially, the hypothesis includes modular validation of individual components (homology mapping quality, adversarial alignment fidelity, contrastive learning utility) and proposes examining transformer attention weights to identify key homologous genes driving cross-species alignment, providing biological interpretability.

**Priority:** Highest. This hypothesis addresses a specific, named failure mode of existing methods, proposes a modular architecture enabling incremental validation, and provides clear falsification conditions. The experimental design explicitly tests robustness to missing modalities and generalization to unseen cell types. However, the hypothesis shares the field-wide blind spot of assuming high-quality homology mapping is available, and should be extended to include explicit degradation analyses when homology quality drops.

### Hypothesis 2: Evolutionary Conservation of Cell-Type-Specific Regulatory Grammars via Hierarchical Multi-Task Learning on Gene Programs
**Elo: 1534**

**Key Claim:** Cell-type identity is determined by the activity of evolutionarily conserved gene programs—modular sets of co-regulated genes performing specific biological functions—rather than by individual gene expression levels. A hierarchical model that first learns a dictionary of such programs from multi-species data in an unsupervised manner, then uses program usage vectors as features for cell-type classification, will enable robust cross-species annotation transfer, including to cell types not present in the training species.

**Scientific Basis:** This hypothesis is grounded in the well-established biological concept that gene programs (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation, cell cycle, immune signaling pathways) represent fundamental functional units of cell identity that are conserved even when individual gene sequences and expression levels diverge across species. While methods like GLUE and CAME integrate multi-omics and cross-species data, they still operate at the gene level. Self-supervised learning approaches (scBERT, TransAnno-Net) capture meaningful biological structure but do not explicitly factorize it into interpretable programs. The proposed two-stage architecture—a variational autoencoder with sparse factor loading matrix for program discovery, regularized by phylogenetic conservation, followed by a classifier operating on program usage—shifts the annotation unit from individual genes to functional modules. This mechanistically distinct approach posits that invariant information for cross-species transfer resides at the program level, not the gene level.

**Experimental Approach:** The model will be pre-trained on large multi-species atlases (Tabula Sapiens, Tabula Muris, zebrafish, Drosophila) with a conservation regularizer that penalizes distance between program loading vectors for orthologous gene groups, weighted by phylogenetic distance. Evaluation uses leave-one-species-out cross-validation with comparisons against scANVI, CAME, and scBERT. A critical test is generalization to unseen cell types: removing certain cell types from training species and evaluating whether the model correctly classifies them in the target species based on program usage patterns. Biological interpretability will be assessed through gene set enrichment analysis of learned programs and examination of their conservation across species.

**Priority:** High. This hypothesis offers the most mechanistically novel contribution by reconceptualizing the annotation problem at the level of gene programs rather than individual genes. Its strength lies in biological grounding and the explicit claim that generalization to unseen cell types is possible when those types utilize known programs in novel combinations. The hierarchical structure enables independent validation of program discovery and classification components. However, the hypothesis needs stronger specification of how the conservation regularizer handles the reality that gene programs themselves evolve and rewire, and should incorporate evolutionary rate heterogeneity across functional modules.

### Hypothesis 3: Cross-Species Annotation via Phylogenetic Gene Program Embeddings and Hyperbolic Manifold Alignment (PhyloCell)
**Elo: 1267**

**Key Claim:** Projecting single-cell transcriptomes into a shared hyperbolic space that naturally captures hierarchical phylogenetic relationships among species will yield better alignment of cell types across species than Euclidean methods, especially for distantly related species. Hyperbolic geometry accommodates the exponential growth of cell-type diversity along the tree of life, enabling better separation of fine subtypes and generalization to unseen species.

**Scientific Basis:** The mathematical motivation is sound: hyperbolic space embeds tree-like structures with exponentially lower distortion than Euclidean space, and cell-type evolution follows a hierarchical tree structure. Existing methods (CAME, scVI, scANVI) operate in Euclidean space, which cannot efficiently represent the exponential branching of species trees. By embedding cells in a Poincaré ball with a tree-structured prior reflecting known species phylogeny, PhyloCell explicitly models evolutionary distance as a geometric prior. The framework first constructs a multi-species gene program dictionary via non-negative matrix factorization with orthology-based program linkage, then uses a hyperbolic variational autoencoder for cell embedding. Cell-type annotation transfer is performed via hyperbolic k-nearest neighbors or hyperbolic logistic regression.

**Experimental Approach:** Testing spans human, mouse, macaque, and zebrafish atlases with phylogenetic tree priors from TimeTree. Evaluation compares macro F1 and accuracy against CAME, SAMap, scVI+scArches, and Seurat v3. Additional experiments assess batch correction, robustness to missing modalities via product-of-experts inference, and zero-shot generalization to unseen species (chicken) without fine-tuning. Latent space interpretability will be assessed by correlating dimensions with gene program scores.

**Priority:** Medium-High. The theoretical motivation is elegant and the use of hyperbolic geometry for phylogenetic modeling is well-justified. However, the hypothesis needs to demonstrate that hyperbolic embeddings provide practical advantages over well-regularized Euclidean methods, rather than assuming that mathematical optimality translates to empirical improvement. The experimental design appropriately includes direct comparisons against Euclidean baselines, which will be critical for validating the core claim. The hypothesis would benefit from specifying failure modes—when would hyperbolic geometry not help?—and from incorporating cell-type-specific evolutionary rates, as immune cells and neurons evolve at dramatically different tempos.

### Hypothesis 4: Cross-Species Annotation via Transfer Learning with Species-Invariant Latent Spaces
**Elo: 1152**

**Key Claim:** A framework integrating self-supervised Transformer pretraining, graph neural networks, and adversarial domain adaptation can learn species-invariant latent spaces enabling robust cross-species cell-type annotation transfer. The combination of these established techniques, with pathway-level interpretability constraints, will outperform linear models and single-species deep learning methods.

**Scientific Basis:** This hypothesis correctly identifies that existing methods either achieve cross-tissue annotation within a single species (scTab) or focus on species-specific transfer learning (TransAnno-Net), leaving a gap in cross-species transfer. The proposed components—Transformer pretraining for universal gene expression patterns, GNNs for cell-cell similarity propagation, adversarial training for species invariance, and semi-supervised fine-tuning—are individually validated in other contexts. The novelty lies in their integration for cross-species single-cell annotation with interpretability constraints.

**Experimental Approach:** The evaluation plan includes multi-species atlas assembly, self-supervised pretraining via masked gene prediction, species-aware graph construction using ortholog expression similarity, adversarial domain classification with gradient reversal, and semi-supervised cell-type classification. Benchmarking against scTab, scANVI, and linear models on held-out species pairs is proposed, along with ablation studies to quantify individual component contributions.

**Priority:** Medium. While the proposed integration is reasonable, this hypothesis exemplifies the "complexity stacking" pattern identified in the meta-review. It combines multiple established techniques without clearly articulating what specific failure mode of existing cross-species methods this particular combination resolves. The adversarial component, in particular, is invoked uncritically without addressing the well-documented risk of destroying biological signal along with batch effects. The hypothesis would be strengthened by specifying a minimal sufficient configuration and by including explicit negative controls that distinguish true cross-species generalization from memorization of shared cell types.

### Hypothesis 5: Cross-Species Cell Type Annotation via Disentangled Topic Alignment (CDTAN)
**Elo: 1046**

**Key Claim:** A two-stage framework combining a disentangled variational autoencoder topic model with a graph neural network alignment module can achieve accurate cross-species cell-type annotation by explicitly separating conserved biological programs from species-specific confounders, then aligning cells based on their topic proportions.

**Scientific Basis:** CODAL demonstrated that mutual information regularization can disentangle biological and technical effects in single-species multi-batch integration, producing batch-corrected latent spaces. CDTAN extends this disentanglement principle to cross-species settings and adds a GNN alignment module. The topic model component ensures interpretability, as each topic corresponds to a gene program with inspectable gene loadings. The GNN alignment module leverages graph structure in topic space to map similar cell types across species, even with limited labeled pairs.

**Experimental Approach:** Validation uses multi-species atlases with known cell-type homologies, comparing annotation accuracy against CIForm, scBERT, and an ablation without disentanglement. Additional experiments test robustness to simulated technical noise, generalization to unseen cell types, latent topic interpretability via gene set enrichment, scalability to million-cell datasets, and performance with missing modalities.

**Priority:** Medium-Low. The disentanglement objective addresses a genuine challenge—species-specific effects confound cross-species alignment—and the use of topic models provides inherent interpretability advantages over black-box transformers. However, the hypothesis does not clearly differentiate itself from Hypothesis 2 (which also uses program factorization but with a hierarchical multi-task structure and explicit phylogenetic regularization) or Hypothesis 4 (which also uses adversarial alignment but with Transformer pretraining). The GNN alignment module assumes that cell types form clusters in topic space that can be aligned via graph attention, but does not address cases where homologous cell types have diverged in their program usage. The hypothesis would benefit from explicit positioning against the gene program factorization approach and from specifying the conditions under which topic-space alignment succeeds or fails.

## Recommended Research Priorities

### Priority 1: Systematic Evaluation of Homology Mapping Quality Impact on Annotation Transfer
**Justification:** The meta-review identifies over-reliance on unvalidated homology assumptions as the most critical blind spot across all hypotheses. No existing work systematically characterizes how annotation accuracy degrades as homology quality decreases, yet the hardest and most practically important cross-species cases are precisely those where homology is least reliable. This experiment should be conducted first because its results will inform which architectural choices are robust to realistic homology uncertainty.

**Specific Experiment:** Using the CrossAnn framework (Hypothesis 1), systematically degrade homology mapping quality by: (a) randomly removing increasing fractions of known orthologs, (b) introducing synthetic many-to-many mappings of varying accuracy, (c) using increasingly distant outgroup species where orthology assignment confidence is low. Measure annotation accuracy (F1, ARI) as a function of homology quality for human-mouse, human-zebrafish, and human-Drosophila pairs. Compare the homology-aware graph transformer against a baseline using only high-confidence one-to-one orthologs. This will establish empirical boundaries for when complex homology modeling provides benefits versus when simpler approaches suffice.

### Priority 2: Head-to-Head Comparison of Gene-Level vs. Program-Level Annotation Transfer
**Justification:** Hypotheses 1 and 2 represent fundamentally different mechanistic claims about where invariant information for cross-species transfer resides—at the level of individual genes with homology relationships (Hypothesis 1) versus at the level of conserved gene programs (Hypothesis 2). Resolving which mechanism (or combination) provides superior transfer, particularly for unseen cell types, is the most consequential theoretical question emerging from this hypothesis set.

**Specific Experiment:** Implement both the CrossAnn graph transformer (Hypothesis 1) and the hierarchical program factorization model (Hypothesis 2) on identical training and evaluation data. Design a critical test: hold out specific cell types entirely from the training species and measure zero-shot annotation accuracy in the target species. Hypothesis 2 predicts that program-level transfer will succeed when the unseen cell type uses known programs in novel combinations; Hypothesis 1 predicts that homology-based gene-level alignment will capture similarity even without explicit program factorization. Include a combined model that uses program usage vectors as node features in the heterogeneous graph to test whether the mechanisms are complementary.

### Priority 3: Asymmetric Transfer from Data-Rich to Data-Poor Species
**Justification:** The meta-review identifies the absence of asymmetric transfer design as a critical gap—real-world annotation needs involve transferring from well-characterized species (human, mouse) to sparsely sampled species, not symmetric transfer between equally well-sampled pairs. No hypothesis addresses this practically most important use case.

**Specific Experiment:** Train models on the full Human Cell Atlas and Mouse Cell Atlas, then test annotation transfer to species with systematically reduced training data (e.g., 100%, 50%, 10%, 1%, and 0% of available zebrafish or macaque data). Measure how performance degrades as target species data decreases, and identify the minimum target-species data needed for reliable annotation. Compare few-shot fine-tuning strategies (linear probing, full fine-tuning, adapter layers) for adapting pre-trained models to new species with limited labeled examples. This experiment directly addresses the real-world scenario facing comparative biologists.

### Priority 4: Detection of Species-Specific Cell Types (Negative Control)
**Justification:** All hypotheses assume that cross-species cell-type homology exists and can be recovered, but some cell types are genuinely species-specific innovations (e.g., human-specific brain cell types, immune innovations in particular lineages). No proposal includes a mechanism for detecting when a query cell type has no meaningful counterpart in the reference, which is essential for avoiding forced misannotation.

**Specific Experiment:** Construct a test set containing both homologous cell types (present in both species) and species-specific cell types (present only in one species, validated by independent biological evidence). Evaluate whether each method can: (a) correctly annotate homologous types, (b) identify species-specific types as "no match" rather than forcing them into reference categories, (c) provide confidence scores that distinguish true matches from spurious alignments. This requires extending each framework with an explicit "unknown" or "novel" class detection mechanism, which will also reveal which architectural choices naturally support open-set recognition.

### Priority 5: Incorporation of Cell-Type-Specific Evolutionary Rate Priors
**Justification:** All hypotheses treat cross-species alignment as a uniform problem, but immune cells evolve rapidly while neurons are deeply conserved, and transcription factors diverge at different rates than metabolic enzymes. Incorporating evolutionary rate heterogeneity is a biologically essential refinement that no current proposal addresses.

**Specific Experiment:** Augment the top-performing model from Priority 2 with phylogenetic branch-length information and gene-family-specific evolutionary rate estimates (obtainable from databases like ENSEMBL Compara or PAML analyses). Implement a cell-type-specific conservation prior that weights the alignment loss differently for rapidly vs. slowly evolving cell types. Test whether this prior improves annotation accuracy specifically for immune cell types (which current methods struggle with due to rapid evolution) while maintaining performance on conserved neuronal types. This experiment bridges the gap between evolutionary biology knowledge and deep learning architecture design.

## Key Knowledge Gaps

### Gap 1: The Relationship Between Homology Mapping Quality and Annotation Transfer Performance
While all hypotheses depend critically on homology mapping (whether one-to-one, many-to-many, or program-based), no systematic characterization exists of how annotation accuracy degrades as a function of homology quality. For distant species pairs, orthology assignment is inherently uncertain, yet we lack empirical boundaries for when homology-based methods remain reliable versus when alternative approaches are needed. This gap is particularly acute because the hardest annotation problems are exactly those where homology is least trustworthy.

### Gap 2: The Unit of Conservation for Cell-Type Identity
The field lacks clarity on whether cell-type identity is best captured at the level of individual homologous genes (Hypothesis 1), conserved gene programs (Hypothesis 2), or some intermediate granularity. While gene programs are biologically appealing as functional units, programs themselves evolve—modules rewire, gain and lose genes, and change their regulatory logic. Understanding the level at which conservation is most robust to evolutionary divergence is a fundamental biological question with direct architectural implications.

### Gap 3: Asymmetric Transfer and Few-Shot Adaptation
All current methods assume roughly symmetric data availability between source and target species, but the practical need is overwhelmingly asymmetric: transferring annotations from data-rich model organisms (human, mouse) to data-poor non-model species. We lack systematic understanding of how performance degrades as target species data decreases, what minimum data is required for reliable transfer, and which architectural features (pretraining, meta-learning, adapter modules) best support few-shot adaptation.

### Gap 4: Detection and Handling of Evolutionary Innovations
No method currently addresses the reality that some cell types are genuine evolutionary innovations without homologs in other species. The field lacks mechanisms for distinguishing "this cell type has no match" from "the alignment failed," which is essential for avoiding forced misannotation in real applications. This requires moving from closed-set to open-set annotation paradigms.

### Gap 5: Interaction Between Technical and Biological Variation in Cross-Species Comparisons
Cross-species single-cell comparisons typically involve data generated with different experimental protocols, read depths, and gene capture efficiencies. How technical variation interacts with biological variation to affect cross-species alignment is poorly understood, yet it confounds all proposed methods. The field needs systematic characterization of whether adversarial domain alignment, batch correction, or other techniques adequately address cross-technology effects or whether these effects are confounded with species-specific biological signals.

### Gap 6: Evolutionary Rate Heterogeneity Across Cell Types and Gene Families
All existing methods treat cross-species alignment as a uniform problem, but the tempo of evolution varies dramatically across cell types (immune cells evolve rapidly, neurons are deeply conserved) and gene families (transcription factors vs. metabolic enzymes). Incorporating this heterogeneity—through cell-type-specific conservation priors, gene-family-aware homology weighting, or phylogenetic branch-length information—remains entirely unexplored in deep learning frameworks for single-cell annotation.

## Cross-Cutting Themes

### Theme 1: The Tension Between Domain Alignment and Biological Signal Preservation
Three of five hypotheses employ adversarial domain alignment to remove species-specific variation, yet the meta-review identifies a critical unresolved tension: adversarial training can destroy biological signal along with batch effects. No hypothesis provides a principled mechanism to preserve meaningful biological variation while removing species-specific artifacts. This tension manifests architecturally as a trade-off between alignment strength and biological fidelity, and resolving it likely requires moving beyond standard adversarial approaches toward methods that explicitly model which dimensions of variation are species-specific versus biologically conserved. The gene program factorization approach (Hypothesis 2) implicitly addresses this by operating at a level where biological signal is presumed to be more robust, but does not provide explicit guarantees.

### Theme 2: The Shift from Gene-Level to Program-Level Representations
Hypotheses 2, 3, and 5 all propose representing cells not as vectors of individual gene expression values, but as mixtures of gene programs—modular sets of co-regulated genes with specific biological functions. This shift is motivated by the biological intuition that programs are more evolutionarily conserved than individual genes, and by the practical advantage that program-level representations are lower-dimensional, more interpretable, and potentially more robust to species-specific expression differences. However, the hypotheses differ in how programs are defined (matrix factorization, topic models, variational autoencoders), how they are linked across species (orthology, co-expression conservation, hyperbolic geometry), and whether programs are learned jointly with the annotation task or separately.

### Theme 3: The Role of Phylogenetic Information in Deep Learning Architectures
Hypothesis 3 explicitly incorporates phylogenetic tree structure through hyperbolic geometry, while Hypothesis 2 uses phylogenetic distance to weight conservation regularization. The broader question is whether and how evolutionary history should constrain deep learning models for cross-species analysis.
